* {{IPA|/m, p, b/}} are bilabial, whereas {{IPA|/f, ʋ, pf/}} are labiodental.<ref name="skp135" />
* {{IPA|/t, d, ts, dz, s, z/}} are dental {{IPA|[{{IPAplink|t̪}}, {{IPAplink|d̪}}, {{IPAplink|t̪s̪}}, {{IPAplink|d̪z̪}}, {{IPAplink|s̪}}, {{IPAplink|z̪}}]}};<ref name="PreTok21">{{Harvcoltxt|Pretnar|Tokarz|1980|p=21}}</ref> i.e., {{IPA|/t, d/}} are [[Laminal consonant|laminal]] [[Denti-alveolar consonant|denti-alveolar]], while {{IPA|/ts, dz, s, z/}} are dentalized laminal [[Alveolar consonant|alveolar]], pronounced with the blade of the tongue very close to the upper front teeth, with the tip of the tongue resting behind the lower front teeth.
* {{IPA|/n, l, ɾ/}} are alveolar.<ref name="PreTok21" /> The first two are laminal denti-alveolar {{IPA|[{{IPAplink|n̪}}, {{IPAplink|l̪}}]}} before dental consonants. In addition, {{IPA|/n/}} is velar {{IPAblink|ŋ}} before velar consonants,<ref name="PreTok21" /><ref name="skp136">{{Harvcoltxt|Šuštaršič|Komar|Petek|1999|p=136}}</ref> and it merges with {{IPA|/m/}} to a labiodental {{IPAblink|ɱ}} before labiodental consonants.<ref name="skp136" />
* It is debatable whether the Slovene {{Angle bracket|r}} should be transcribed as {{IPA|/ɾ/}}<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Šuštaršič|Komar|Petek|1999|pp=135–136}}</ref> or {{IPA|/r/}}.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Greenberg|2006|pp=17–20}}</ref><ref name="PreTok21"/><ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Šekli|2014|p=20}}</ref> The pronunciation varies between native speakers, but generally they all oppose 'typical' long trill.
* {{IPA|/ɾ/}} is [[Uvular consonant|uvular]] in a number of [[Upper Carniolan dialect group|Upper Carniolan]] and [[Carinthian dialect group|Carinthian]] dialects, but such pronunciation is not allowed in Standard Slovene.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Reindl|2008|pp=56–57}}</ref>
* {{IPA|/k/}} is usually pronounced a bit more aspirated and it becomes fully aspirated {{IPA|/kʰ/}} before close vowels.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|p=96}}</ref>
* {{IPA|/g/}} is [[spirantize]]d into {{IPA|/ɣ/}} in a large portion of the Slovene-speaking area (all [[Littoral dialect group|Littoral dialects]], most [[Rovte dialect group|Rovte dialects]], some parts of the [[Upper Carniolan dialect group|Upper Carniolan dialects]], the western [[Carinthian dialect group|Carinthian dialects]]), or even further into {{IPA|/χ, ʁ/}} or even disappeared altogether.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Logar|1996|pp=7, 30–35}}</ref>
* {{IPA|/ɾ/}} is usually described as the sequence {{IPA|/əɾ/}} (with an epenthetic {{IPA|[ə]}}). Jones (2002){{fix|text=full citation needed|date=December 2016}} found that a vocalic segment similar to {{IPA|[ə]}} occurs before (and occasionally after) both syllabic and non-syllabic {{IPA|/ɾ/}}, and that it is shorter than epenthetic {{IPA|[ə]}}, leading to the conclusion that this is not epenthetic {{IPA|[ə]}}, but simply a feature of rhotic consonant production in Slovene.
* {{IPA|/pf/}}, {{IPA|/kx/}}, {{IPA|/dʒ/}} and {{IPA|/dz/}} as a phoneme only appear in loanwords, such as ''pfenig'' {{IPA|[ˈpféːnìk]}} '[[pfennig]]', ''sikh'' {{IPA|[ˈsîːkx]}} '[[Sikhs|Sikh]]', ''džez'' {{IPA|[ˈdʒɛ̂s]}} 'jazz' and ''dzeta'' {{IPA|[ˈdzéːtà]}} '[[zeta]]'.<ref name=":5">{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|p=97}}</ref>

{{IPA|/ʋ/}} has several [[allophones]] depending on context.

* Before a vowel, the pronunciation is labiodental, {{IPAblink|ʋ}}.<ref name="skp136" />
* Before or after a vowel, the pronunciation is bilabial {{IPAblink|u̯}} and forms a diphthong.<ref name="skp136" /><ref name="gr18">{{Harvcoltxt|Greenberg|2006|p=18}}</ref><ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2001|p=138}}</ref>
* At the beginning of a syllable, before a consonant (for example in ''vsi'' 'all'), the pronunciation varies more widely by speaker and area. Some speakers convert {{IPA|/ʋ/}} into a full vowel {{IPA|[u]}} in this position.<ref name="skp136" /><ref name="gr18" /> For those speakers that retain a consonantal pronunciation, it pre-labializes the following consonant.<ref name="skp136" /><ref name="gr18" /><ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|p=95}}</ref> Thus, ''vsi'' may be pronounced as disyllabic {{IPA|[uˈsî]}} or monosyllabic {{IPA|[ˈʷsî]}}.
* In some dialects {{IPA|/ʋ/}} turned into {{IPA|/v/}} instead of {{IPA|[u̯]}}/{{IPA|[w]}}/{{IPA|[ᵂ]}} and devoices as a normal obstruent (see consonant changes), so ''vsi'' would in those dialects be pronounced {{IPA|[ˈfsî]}}.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Logar|1996|pp=8, 30}}</ref>

The preposition ''v'' is always bound to the following word and it is always pronounced as {{IPA|/u̯/}} except when standing alone out of a sentence (the pronunciation then is {{IPA|[úː]}}).<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2001|p=1634}}</ref>

Under certain (somewhat unpredictable) circumstances, historical {{IPA|/l/}} at the end of a syllable has become {{IPA|[u̯]}} (or {{IPA|/w/}} after {{IPA|/ɾ/}}). This change has occurred in the endings of all past participles ending in vowel + ''l''. For many derivatives of words ending in {{IPA|[u̯]}} that historically had {{IPA|/l/}}, both {{IPA|[l]}} and {{IPA|[u̯]}} can be used, sometimes depending on the context it is being used in.

{{IPA|/p/}} and {{IPA|/b/}} have different pronunciations before some sonorants:<ref name=":0">{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2001|p=76}}</ref>

* Before {{IPA|/m/}}, pronunciation is nasal, {{IPA|[pⁿ]}} and {{IPA|[bⁿ]}}, respectively.
* Before {{IPA|/f/}} and {{IPA|/ʋ/}}, pronunciation is labiodental, {{IPA|[p̪]}} and {{IPA|[b̪]}}, respectively.

Similarly, {{IPA|/t/}} and {{IPA|/d/}} also change their pronunciations:<ref name=":0" />

* Before {{IPA|/n/}}, the pronunciation is nasal, {{IPA|[tⁿ]}} and {{IPA|[dⁿ]}}, respectively.
* Before {{IPA|/l/}}, they become lateral stops, {{IPA|[tˡ]}} and {{IPA|[dˡ]}}, respectively.

=== Bigger dialectal variations ===
Slovene has many dialects, which have their own consonant changes. Some of the changes that impact only one consonant are already listed above and more general changes that impact more consonants, as well as two distinctions that are not present in standard language anymore are listed here.

==== Palatal sonorants ====
[[Slovene dialects#Unification|Alpine Slovene]], the transitional language between [[Proto-Slavic language|Proto-Slavic]] and Slovene, had four (alveolo-)palatal sonorants: j {{IPA|/j/}}, ľ {{IPA|/lʲ/}}, ń {{IPA|/nʲ/}}, and ŕ {{IPA|/ɾʲ/}}.<ref name=":12">{{Harvcoltxt|Logar|1996|p=30}}</ref> Sonorants {{IPA|/lʲ/}}, {{IPA|/nʲ/}} and {{IPA|/ɾʲ/}} all turned into sequences {{IPA|/lj/}}, {{IPA|/nj/}} and {{IPA|/rj/}}, respectively if followed by a vowel. Before a consonant, {{IPA|/ɾʲ/}} merged with {{IPA|/ɾ/}} in all dialects, while {{IPA|/lʲ/}} still retains its palatal pronunciation in [[Upper Savinja dialect|Upper Savinja]], [[Inner Carniolan dialect|Inner Carniolan]], [[Karst dialect|Karst]], [[Soča dialect|Soča]], [[Istrian dialect|Istrian]], [[Upper Carniolan dialect|Upper Carniolan]] around [[Bohinj]], [[South White Carniolan dialect|South White Carniolan]], [[Kostel dialect|Kostel]] and southern part of [[Gail Valley dialect|Gail Valley]] dialects and {{IPA|/nʲ/}} retains the pronunciation in [[Resian dialect|Resian]], [[Torre Valley dialect|Torre Valley]], [[Natisone Valley dialect|Natisone Valley]], [[Karst dialect|Karst]], [[Inner Carniolan dialect|Inner Carniolan]], [[Istrian dialect|Istrian]], [[Brda dialect|Brda]], [[Soča dialect|Soča]], [[Gail Valley dialect|Gail Valley]], [[Rosen Valley dialect|Rosen Valley]], [[Upper Carniolan dialect|Upper Carniolan]] around [[Bohinj]], [[Prekmurje Slovene|Prekmurje]], [[Kostel dialect|Kostel]] and [[South White Carniolan dialect|South White Carniolan]] dialects. In other dialects they either merged with their non-palatal counterparts, merged with {{IPA|/j/}} or turned into sequences {{IPA|/jl/}} and {{IPA|/jn/}} ({{IPA|/j̃/}}).<ref name=":12" /> Therefore, Standard Slovene allows three different pronunciations in this case:<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2001|p=136}}</ref><ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2001|p=137}}</ref>

* Speakers of dialects which retained original pronunciation usually pronounce them as {{IPA|/lʲ/}} and {{IPA|/nʲ/}}, respectively. Elsewhere, this pronunciation is considered archaic.
* Speakers of dialects which completely lost the distinction between palatal and non-palatal {{IPA|/l/}} and {{IPA|/n/}} pronounce them the same also in the Standard language.
* Other speakers can pronounce them either as one of the forms above, or as longer {{IPA|/lː/}} and {{IPA|/nː/}}, respectively.

In the orthography, former {{IPA|/lʲ/}}, {{IPA|/nʲ/}} and {{IPA|/ɾʲ/}} are always represented by the sequences {{Angle bracket|lj}}, {{Angle bracket|nj}} and {{Angle bracket|rj}}, respectively, except before a consonant {{IPA|/ɾʲ/}} is represented by {{Angle bracket|r}}.

==== tʼ–č distinction ====
The tʼ–č distinction is one of the two distinctions that was present in Alpine Slovene and is still present in some dialects, but is not present in the standard language anymore. Dialects that still have this distinction ([[Resian dialect|Resian]] and [[Torre Valley dialect|Torre Valley]] dialects, some [[Soča dialect|Soča]], [[Inner Carniolan dialect|Inner Carniolan]] and [[Istrian dialect|Istrian]] microdialects)<ref name=":12" /> mostly do not pronounce the phoneme as {{IPA|/c/}} anymore, but as {{IPA|/tɕ/}}, which was also the standard pronunciation during the [[Reformation]]. Therefore, this distinction is either dialectal or obsolete. In other dialects, {{IPA|/c/}} merged with {{IPA|/tʃ/}}. Examples, where the difference can be observed are ''sveča'' {{IPA|[ˈsʋèːtɕá]}} (with distinction) {{IPA|[ˈsʋèːtʃá]}} (without distinction) 'candle', ''teči'' {{IPA|[ˈtɛ̀ːtɕí]}} (with distinction) {{IPA|[ˈtɛ̀ːtʃí]}} (without distinction) 'to run (to flow)' and ''hočem'' {{IPA|[ˈxòːtɕɛ́m]}} (with distinction) {{IPA|[ˈxòːtʃɛ́m]}} (without distinction) '(I) want'.

==== Shvapanye, elkanye, vekanye and *ł ====
In Alpine Slavic, *ł was an allophone of *l before consonants, back vowels and {{IPA|/a/}}, and before a stop.<ref name=":12" /> Some dialects still retain the original pronunciation (e.g., [[Horjul dialect|Horjul]], [[Lower Carniolan dialect|Lower Carniolan]], [[Inner Carniolan dialect|Inner Carniolan]] and [[Upper Savinja dialect|Upper Savinja]] dialects. Shvapanye ({{Langx|sl|švapanje}}) is the pronunciation of {{IPA|/l/}} and {{IPA|/ʋ/}} as {{IPA|[w]}} or {{IPA|[u̯]}} before all back vowels, consonants and {{IPA|/a/}} ({{IPA|/ʌ/}}), which is present in the [[Carinthian dialect group|Carinthian dialects]], [[Upper Carniolan dialect]], some northern [[Lower Carniolan dialect|Lower Carniolan microdialects]] and the [[Čabranka dialect]].<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Logar|1996|p=8}}</ref> In standard language, shvapanye is only somewhat present, as described above. Elkanye ({{Langx|sl|elkanje}}) and vekanye ({{Langx|sl|vekanje}}) is the hypercorrect way of pronouncing words, without shvapanye.

==== Slekanye ====
Slekanye ({{Langx|sl|slekanje}}) is a phenomenon mostly limited to slovenized Germans, which live in around [[Rut, Tolmin|Rut]] ([[Bača subdialect]]) and is the merge of alveolar and post-alveolar fricatives and affricates into one phoneme, which is pronounced somewhere in between ({{IPA|/t͇s͇/}}, {{IPA|/s͇/}} and {{IPA|/z͇/}} for {{IPA|/ts/}} and {{IPA|/tʃ/}}, {{IPA|/s/}} and {{IPA|/ʃ/}}, and {{IPA|/z/}} and {{IPA|/ʒ/}}, respectively).<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Logar|1996|p=9}}</ref> Apart from that area, it is also known for a part of the Torre Valley dialect.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Ježovnik|2019|pp=238–239}}</ref>

==== tl/dl–t/d distinction ====
Proto-Slavic consonant clusters *tl and *dl simplified quite soon in central, southern and eastern areas while it disappeared later from western and northern dialects. Today, the only dialect that still retains this distinction is the [[Gail Valley dialect]]. The clusters ''tl'' and ''dl'' that are nowadays present in the standard language became such after the omission of *ь/*ъ between the consonants. The distinction can be seen in the word ''vile'' {{IPA|[ˈʋìːdlɛ́]}} (Gail Valley dialect without any other dialectal changes) {{IPA|[ˈʋìːlɛ́]}} (all other dialects).

==== Dissimilation ====
When a stop or affricate is followed by another stop or affricate, it [[Dissimilation (phonology)|dissimilate]]s into a fricative, e. g. ''beda'''k''''' {{IPA|[beˈdǎːk]}} 'idiot' + ''-ski'' {{IPA|[ski]}} → ''beda'''š'''ki'' {{IPA|[beˈdàːʃkí]}} 'idiotic' and '''''k''''' {{IPA|[k]}} 'to' + ''grobu'' {{IPA|[ˈgrɔ̀ːbú]}} 'grave' → ''h grobu'' {{IPA|[x‿ˈgɾɔ̀ːbú]}} 'to the grave'.

In this context, {{IPA|[v]}} and {{IPA|[ɣ]}} may occur as voiced allophones of {{IPA|/f/}} and {{IPA|/x/}}, respectively (e.g., ''vŕh drevésa'' {{IPA|[ˈʋə̂ɾɣ dɾeˈʋéːsà]}} 'top of a tree'),<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Herrity|2000|p=16}}</ref> while {{IPA|[gɣ]}} and {{IPA|[bv]}} would be hardly ever used allophones of {{IPA|/kx/}} and {{IPA|/pf/}}, respectively. Consonant clusters in non-assimilated words can be excluded from this rule, for example ''podcast'' {{IPA|[ˈpóːdˌkàst]}} 'podcast'.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|p=98}}</ref>

When a dental/alevolar fricative or affricate are followed by a postalveolar fricative, affricate or {{Angle bracket|nj}}/{{Angle bracket|lj}}/{{Angle bracket|rj}}, they usually become postalveolar; e.g., ''stric Žan'' 'uncle, whose name is Žan' {{IPA|[ˈstrîːdzˈʒâːn]}} or {{IPA|[ˈstrîːdʒˈʒâːn]}} or {{IPA|[ˈstrîːˈdʒːâːn]}}.<ref name=":1" />

Nasal {{IPA|/m/}}, {{IPA|/n/}} also match the place of articulation with the following consonant: ''Istanbul'' '[[Istanbul]]' {{IPA|[ˈíːstamˌbùl]}}, ''informacija'' 'information' {{IPA|[ˌiɱfoɾˈmàːtsijà]}}, ''banka'' 'bank' {{IPA|[ˈbáːŋkà]}} (but they do not change articulation before post-alveolar consonants and {{IPA|/m/}} also does not have allophone {{IPA|[ŋ]}}).<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2000|p=153}}</ref>

==== Gemination of consonants ====
Several consonant clusters also get simplified into geminated consonants. In fast speech, however, they change into usual, non-geminated consonants.<ref name=":1">{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2001|pages=79–80}}</ref>
* When two or (rarely) more of the same consonants are pronounced one after another, they become geminated, taking the allophone of the first one; e.g., ''brezzob'' 'toothless' {{IPA|[breˈzːôp]}}. Stops and affricates can be geminated or pronounced separately; e.g., ''oddati'' 'to hand in' {{IPA|[odˈdàːtí]}} or {{IPA|[oˈdːàːtí]}}. Labialized consonants can also lose labialization after {{IPA|[u̯]}}; e.g., ''siv vzorec'' 'gray pattern' {{IPA|[ˈsîːu̯ˈʷzóːɾə̀ts]}} or {{IPA|[ˈsîːˈu̯ːzóːɾə̀ts]}} (of course ''vzorec'' can also be acute).
* When a dental/alveolar stop is followed by a dental/alveolar affricate, then they can be pronounced separately or combine into a geminated affricate; e.g., ''od čebele'' 'from a bee' {{IPA|[ʔottʃeˈbéːlɛ̀]}} or {{IPA|[ʔotʃːeˈbéːlɛ̀]}}.
* When a dental/alveolar stop or affricate is followed by a dental/alveolar fricative, they combine into a geminated affricate or are pronounced separately; e.g., ''podse'' 'under itself' {{IPA|[ˈpóːtsɛ̀]}} or {{IPA|[ˈpóːtsːɛ̀]}}.

* Stressed front vowels are in most words pronounced as lax {{IPAblink|ɪ}} when {{IPA|/r/}} follows, so that, e.g., ''mira'' 'myrrh' is pronounced {{IPA|[ˈmɪ̀ːɾá]}} and ''večer'' 'evening' is pronounced {{IPA|[ʋɛˈt͡ʃɪ̂ːɾ]}}, but loanwords are exceptions, such as ''virus'' {{IPA|[ˈʋíːɾus]}} and ''vera'' {{IPA|[ˈʋɛ́ːɾa]}}.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007a|p=3}}</ref><ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2001|p=139}}</ref><ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|p=63}}</ref>

* Vowels {{IPA|/ɛ/}}, {{IPA|/ɔ/}}, {{IPA|/ɪ/}} and {{IPA|/a/}} (but not {{IPA|/ʌ/}}) are pronounced with retracted tongue root while others with advanced tongue root.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|p=36}}</ref>

Jurgec proposes the existence of a ninth vowel {{IPA|/ʌ/}} in tonemic variety that in traditional pronunciation (see below under [[Slovene phonology#Prosody|Prosody]]) would rather be analyzed as a short {{IPA|/a/}}. However, since more recent studies indicate that native speakers do not actually phonemically distinguish long and short vowels and yet the distinction between {{IPA|/ʌ/}} and {{IPA|/a/}} is quite consistently perceived by tonal speakers, and moreover there is a noticeable distinction in quality and a lesser distinction in quantity between these two vowels, there is reason to treat these two sounds as two different phonemes.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2011|p=139}}</ref>

The near-open {{IPA|/ʌ/}} can only appear in the word-final stressed syllable before the syllable coda, as in ''čas'' {{IPA|[ˈtʃʌ̂s]}} 'time'. Due to the restrictions stated above, the open {{IPA|/a/}} usually appears in its place in other declinational forms of the same word: ''časa'' {{IPA|[ˈtʃàːsá]}}, not {{IPA|[ˈtʃʌ̀ːsá]}}, 'time (gen.)'. The analysis as two different phonemes is also reinforced by the fact that in some words the phoneme {{IPA|/a/}} appears in the very same position that would permit {{IPA|/ʌ/}}, leading to a phonemic contrast: ''pas'' {{IPA|[ˈpâs]}}, not {{IPA|[ˈpʌ̂s]}}, 'belt'.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2011|p=260}}</ref>

Jurgec also states that in the tonemic varieties of the language, the near-open vowel {{IPA|/ʌ/}} can carry only the high tone (see below), which is "parallel to the pattern for the [{{IPA|/ɛ/}}, {{IPA|/ɔ/}} and {{IPA|/ə/}}]." He also notes that similarly to {{IPA|/ʌ/}}, the schwa {{IPA|/ə/}} likewise only appears in closed syllables; i.e., as the nucleus before the syllable coda. On the basis of these observations he concludes that the near-open vowel {{IPA|/ʌ/}} "behaves in a systematic way within the vowel system of Slovenian."<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2011|p=268}}</ref>

According to {{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007}}, {{IPA|/ə/}} is inserted epenthetically, and its distribution is fully predictable. He also says that "[d]escriptions of schwa distribution are offer[ed] in lexical rather than grammatical terms. These were also based on historical data and did not consider actual speech of educated speakers in Ljubljana, nowadays considered standard."<ref name="jurgec20071">{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007a|p=1}}</ref>

Slovene has been traditionally described as distinguishing vowel length, which correlates with stress and is therefore discussed in the prosody section, below. The distinction between {{IPA|/ɛ/}} and {{IPA|/e/}}, and between {{IPA|/ɔ/}} and {{IPA|/o/}} is only made when they are stressed and long. When short or unstressed, they are not distinguished: short stressed variants are realized as open-mid {{IPA|[{{IPAplink|ɛ}}, {{IPAplink|ɔ}}]}}, while the unstressed variants are, broadly speaking, true-mid vowels {{IPA|[{{IPAplink|e̞}}, {{IPAplink|o̞}}]}}. In fact, however, the unstressed mid vowels have two realizations:

* Lowered close-mid (between close-mid and true-mid) {{IPA|[e̞, o̞]}} before a stressed syllable (as in ''v'''e'''likan'' 'giant' and '''''o'''glas'' 'advertisement').<ref name="srebotrejec">{{Harvcoltxt|Srebot-Rejec|1998}}</ref><ref name="solar54">{{Harvcoltxt|Šolar|1950|p=54}}, cited in {{Harvcoltxt|Srebot-Rejec|1998}}</ref>
* Raised open-mid (between true-mid and open-mid) {{IPA|[ɛ̝, ɔ̝]}} after a stressed syllable (as in ''medv'''e'''d'' 'bear' and ''pot'''o'''k'' 'stream').<ref name="srebotrejec" /><ref name="solar54" />

The unstressed mid vowels are never as close as the stressed close-mid vowels {{IPA|/e, o/}} and never as open as the stressed open-mid vowels {{IPA|/ɛ, ɔ/}}.<ref name="srebotrejec" /> However, {{Harvcoltxt|Šuštaršič|Komar|Petek|1999}} report true-mid allophones {{IPA|[{{IPAplink|e̞}}, {{IPAplink|o̞}}]}} of the close-mid vowels {{IPA|/e, o/}} occurring in the sequences {{IPA|/ej/}} and {{IPA|/ou̯/}}, but only if a vowel does not follow within the same word.<ref name="skp138a">{{Harvcoltxt|Šuštaršič|Komar|Petek|1999|p=138}}</ref><ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2001|pp=140–144}}</ref> One could therefore argue that the unstressed mid vowels are simply allophones of the close-mid vowels, whereas the open-mid vowels do not occur in unstressed positions. Another argument for transcribing the unstressed mid vowels as {{IPA|/e, o/}} is that these symbols are easier to write than {{IPA|/ɛ, ɔ/}}. These allophones have neither advanced nor retracted tongue root.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|p=61}}</ref>

In most cases, unstressed vowels are nowadays written as {{IPA|/e, o/}} before the stress and as {{IPA|/ɛ, ɔ/}} after the stress; however, an older way of writing them as {{IPA|/ɛ, ɔ/}} everywhere is still very common (e.g. in Toporišič 2001).<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Toporišič|2001|pages=139–143}}</ref>

In some loanwords, sonorant clusters may be present that are required to form a new syllable in Slovene. Tonal speakers insert {{IPA|[ə]}}, the same way as happens with sonorant + non-sonorant clusters, but non-tonal speakers form a syllabic consonant, except if the second sonorant is {{IPA|[ɾ]}}; then {{IPA|[ə]}} is inserted in both varieties: ''film'' {{IPA|[ˈfìːlə́m]}} (tonal speakers) {{IPA|[ˈfiːlm̩]}} (non-tonal speakers) 'film, movie'.<ref name=":3" />

When unstressed {{IPA|/ə, i, e/}} are followed by {{IPA|[u̯]}}, they can be pronounced together as {{IPA|[u]}}.<ref name=":1" />

In non-assimilated loanwords, German {{IPA|/yː/}}, {{IPA|/øː/}}, {{IPA|/œ/}}, {{IPA|/ʏ/}} are also allowed (e.g., {{IPA|[ˈmýːnxə̀n]}} '[[Munich]]', {{IPA|[ˈgǿːtɛ̀]}} '[[Johann Wolfgang von Goethe|Goethe]]', {{IPA|[ˈkœ̂ln]}} '[[Cologne]]', {{IPA|[ˈɾaːʋənsˈbrʏ̂k]}} '[[Ravensbrück concentration camp|Ravensbrück]]'). When the word becomes assimilated or any affixes are added, they become vernacularized. The pronunciation, however, varies widely between speakers depending on their knowledge of German.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|pages=158–164}}</ref> Here, the phonemes are given for a really educated person and the first change to go in pronunciation of {{IPA|/ʏ/}} qualitatively different that {{IPA|/yː/}}.

In the colloquial spoken language, unstressed and most short stressed vowels tend to be reduced or elided. For example, ''kȕp'' ('heap') > {{IPA|[kə̂p]}}, ''právimo'' ('we say') > {{IPA|[ˈpɾâu̯mó]}}.<ref name="priestley394">{{Harvcoltxt|Priestley|2002|p=394}}</ref>

=== Syllable breaks ===
Standard Slovene does not really have diphthongs. The closest to a diphthong are combinations of vowel + {{IPA|[u̯]}} or {{IPA|[j]}}. In all other cases, two following vowels form two different syllables, e. g. ''poenostaviti'' 'simplify' {{IPA|[po.ˌenoˈstàːʋiˌtí]}}. Often, these clusters simplify into one vowel (which is reflected in the orthography) or insert {{IPA|[j]}} (always after {{IPA|/i/}}, which is not necessarily reflected in the orthography) or {{IPA|[ʔ]}}.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2004|pages=125–126}}</ref> When two vowels are pronounced one after another, they are usually pronounced as would be expected. Exceptions are unstressed {{IPA|/i/}} and {{IPA|/u̯/}}, which are pronounced as usual or turn into {{IPA|[j]}} and {{IPA|[u̯]}}, respectively if preceded by a vowel, e.g., ''bo imela'' '(she) will have' {{IPA|[bo‿iˈméːlà]}} or {{IPA|[bo‿jˈméːlà]}}.<ref name=":1" />

=== Dialectal variation ===
Number of vowels varies drastically between dialects. For example, [[Tolmin dialect]] has 3 long vowels, while some [[Carinthian dialect group|Carinthian microdialects]] can have 15 or even more long vowels. Alpine Slavic had three distinct long e-like vowels (which are in standard language all represented by {{IPA|/eː/}}) and two distinct long o-like vowels (which are in standard language all represented by {{IPA|/oː/}}. However, most dialects at least differentiate between one e-like vowel and the other two. In fact, Standard Slovene has one of the most simplified vowel systems of all dialects. Also not common for other dialects is that it does not contain any [[diphthong]]s as they all monophthongized. The only vowels that consistently merged with other vowels is *ə̄, which turned into {{IPA|/aː/}} in the west and south (and in standard language) and to {{IPA|/eː/}} in the north and east.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Logar|1996|pp=4–5}}</ref>

While long vowels tend to be diphthongized, short vowels tend to be reduced because of modern vowel reduction, which is also common in colloquial spoken language. Apart from [[Centralization (phonetics)|centralization]], {{IPA|/e/}} is commonly pronounced as {{IPA|/a/}} ([[akanye]]) or as {{IPA|/i/}} (ikanye) and {{IPA|/o/}} is commonly pronounced as {{IPA|/a/}} (akanye) or {{IPA|/u/}} (ukanye).<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Logar|1996|p=5}}</ref>

The most common pronunciation difference between speakers is {{IPA|/e/}} vs. {{IPA|/ɛ/}} and {{IPA|/o/}} vs. {{IPA|/ɔ/}} as the dialectal distribution is inconsistent with the distribution in Standard Slovene. This influences the way speakers of such dialects speak Standard Slovene.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2005|p=11}}</ref>

A new change that is currently happening most notably around Ljubljana is the pronunciation of {{IPA|/ə/}} as {{IPA|[ɛ]}} or {{IPA|[e]}}, eliminating another distinction between vowels.<ref name=":4">{{Harvcoltxt|Rigler|2001|pp=157–158}}</ref>

==== {{IPA|[ˈsɛm]}} vs. {{IPA|[ˈsəm]}} ====
Historically, {{IPA|[ˈsɛm]}} is the pronunciation of ''sem'' in the meaning 'here' and {{IPA|[ˈsəm]}} for ''sem'' in the meaning '(I) am', but due to modern vowel reduction, which is the most prominent in monosyllabic words, and the recent development of {{IPA|/ə/}} → {{IPA|/ɛ/}} , the roles are nowadays mostly switched, i. e. '(I) am' is now pronounced {{IPA|[ˈsɛm]}} and 'here' is now pronounced {{IPA|[ˈsəm]}}.<ref name=":4" />

== Prosody ==

Slovene has free [[Stress (linguistics)|stress]]: stress can occur on any syllable and is not predictable. The same word can be stressed quite differently in different dialects. Most words have a single syllable that carries stress. Some compounds, but not all, have multiple stressed syllables, inherited from the parts that make up the compound. There are also a few small words and clitics, including prepositions, that have no inherent stress at all and attach prosodically to another word.

=== Vowel length ===

Slovene is traditionally analysed as having a distinction between long and short vowels. Stress and vowel length are closely intertwined:<ref name="Priestley 2002 390">{{Harvcoltxt|Priestley|2002|p=390}}</ref>

* A non-final syllable that bears stress will automatically have a long vowel. Conversely, at most one vowel in a Slovene word is long, and it automatically bears the stress.
* If a word has no long vowels, the stress usually falls on the final syllable. However, a limited number of words have non-final stress on short syllables.
* Schwa {{IPA|/ə/}} can also be stressed non-finally, but has no length distinctions.

Vowel length carries a low [[functional load]]: it is distinctive only in stressed final syllables, which can be either long or short. In other syllables, however, whether vowel length or stress, or both, are phonemic depends on the underlying phonological analysis. Generally speaking, stress and length co-occur in all but the final syllable, so one feature or the other is phonetically redundant in those words.

Recently, scholars<ref>For example {{Harvcoltxt|Srebot-Rejec|1988}} and {{Harvcoltxt|Šuštaršič|Komar|Petek|1999}}</ref> have found that vowel length in standard Slovene is no longer distinctive,<ref name="skp136" /><ref name="jurgec20071" /><ref name="srebotrejec" /><ref name="srebotrejec1988">{{Harvcoltxt|Srebot-Rejec|1988}}</ref> and that the only differences in vowel length are that the stressed vowels are longer than the unstressed ones,<ref name="srebotrejec" /><ref name="skp137">{{Harvcoltxt|Šuštaršič|Komar|Petek|1999|p=137}}</ref> and that stressed open syllables are longer than stressed closed syllables.<ref name="srebotrejec" /> Stressed syllables are characterized by amplitude and pitch prominence.<ref name="skp137" />

=== Accent shifts ===
Standard Slovene has undergone two accent shifts since Alpine Slovene. The first one, which happened in the 15th century, is from open short final syllables to the mid-close syllable before in words with two syllables, e. g. ''žena'' 'wife' {{IPA|[ʒeˈnâ]}} → {{IPA|[ˈʒɛ̀ːná]}}. The original accentuation is retained in a part of [[Rosen Valley dialect|Rosen Valley]], [[Resian dialect|Resian]], [[Natisone Valley dialect|Natisone Valley]], [[Torre Valley dialect|Torre Valley]] and southern part of [[Soča dialect|Soča]] dialects and is considered obsolete in Standard Slovene.<ref name=":6">{{Harvcoltxt|Šekli|2018|pp=310–314}}</ref>

The second accent shift was from short final syllable to the mid vowel {{IPA|/ə/}} in the syllable before, e. g. ''megla'' 'fog' {{IPA|[məˈglâ]}} → {{IPA|[ˈmə̀glá]}} (→ {{IPA|[ˈmɛ̀glá]}}). This change did not happen in all the aforementioned dialects, as well as [[Upper Carniolan dialect|Upper]] and [[Lower Carniolan dialect|Lower Carniolan]] dialects. Standard language allows both accents, but the unshifted one is considered archaic or high literary, as with the pronunciation of {{IPA|/ə/}} as {{IPA|[ɛ]}} or {{IPA|[e]}}, the shift occurs also in Upper and Lower Carniolan dialects.<ref name=":6" />

Other two common accent shifts, that are not present in Standard Slovene are from all short final ({{IPA|[ʋiˈsɔ̂k]}} → {{IPA|[ˈʋìːsɔ́k]}}) and from all long circumflex final ({{IPA|[seˈnôː]}} → {{IPA|[ˈsèːnɔ́]}}). The first one happened in [[Tolmin dialect|Tolmin]], [[Cerkno dialect|Cerkno]], [[Črni Vrh dialect|Črni Vrh]], [[Horjul dialect|Horjul]], [[Karst dialect|Karst]], [[Inner Carniolan dialect|Inner Carniolan]], [[Istrian dialect|Istrian]], [[Kostel dialect|Kostel]], [[Čabranka dialect|Čabranka]], [[South White Carniolan dialect|South White Carniolan]] and [[North White Carniolan dialect]]s, as well as all [[Styrian dialect group|Styrian dialects]], except [[Lower Sava Valley dialect]] and the second one happened in [[Gail Valley dialect]] without the [[Kranjska Gora subdialect|subdialect]], [[Resian dialect|Resian]], [[Torre Valley dialect|Torre Valley]], [[Črni Vrh dialect|Črni Vrh]], [[Poljane dialect|Poljane]], eastern part of [[Rosen Valley dialect|Rosen Valley]], [[Jaun Valley dialect|Jaun]], [[Mežica dialect|Mežica]], [[North Pohorje–Remšnik dialect|North Pohorje–Remšnik]], [[Upper Savinja dialect|Upper Savinja]], [[Central Savinja dialect|Central Savinja]], [[South Pohorje dialect|South Pohorje]], [[Kostel dialect|Kostel]], [[Čabranka dialect|Čabranka]], [[North White Carniolan dialect|North White Carniolan]] and [[South White Carniolan dialect|South White Carniolan]] dialects.<ref name=":6" />

=== Tone ===

The standard language has two varieties, tonemic and non-tonemic. Tonemic varieties distinguish between two tones or pitch contours on stressed syllables, while non-tonemic varieties do not make this distinction. The tonemic varieties are found in a north–south band in the center of the country ([[Gail Valley dialect|Gail Valley]], [[Rosen Valley dialect|Rosen Valley]], [[Ebriach dialect|Ebriach]], most of [[Jaun Valley dialect|Jaun Valley]], [[Natisone Valley dialect|Natisone Valley]], [[Torre Valley dialect|Torre Valley]], [[Soča dialect|Soča]], a lesser part of [[Tolmin dialect|Tolmin]], [[Upper Carniolan dialect|Upper Carniolan]] without the [[Eastern Upper Carniolan subdialect|subdialect]], most of [[Selca dialect|Selca]], [[Horjul dialect|Horjul]], [[Poljane dialect|Poljane]], and [[Lower Carniolan dialect|Lower Carniolan]] dialects, a well as southwestern part of [[South White Carniolan dialect]]).<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Priestley|2002|p=449}}</ref><ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Šekli|2018|pp=319–349}}</ref> Dialects in the eastern and south-western part of Slovenia are non-tonemic. However, because the Slovenian capital city [[Ljubljana]] is located within the central tonemic dialect area, phonemic tone was included in the standard language, and in fact the tonemic variety is more prestigious and is universally used in formal TV and radio broadcasts.

The two tones are:

* A low-pitch contour, also known as "acute". It is indicated with an acute diacritic {{angbr|é}} on long syllables, a grave {{angbr|è}} on short syllables.
* A high-pitch contour, also known as "circumflex". It is indicated with an inverted breve diacritic {{angbr|ȇ}} on long syllables, a double grave {{angbr|ȅ}} on short syllables.

The exact distribution and phonetic realization of tonemes varies locally.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Greenberg|2006|p=22}}</ref> In Standard Slovene, some words may have either an acute or circumflex tone, with the chosen tone differing by speaker.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Greenberg|2006|p=23}}</ref> Unless otherwise noted, this article discusses the tonemes as they are realized in Standard Slovene spoken in Ljubljana.

Tone is differentiated only on the stressed and on the last syllables, where it is the opposite of the tone that stressed syllable has (except in some prepositions). If last syllable is stressed, then they merge and form rising (acute) or falling (circumflex) tone; e.g., ''pot'' {{IPA|[ˈpǒːt]}} / {{IPA|[ˈpôːt]}} 'path'. Other vowels have neutral (mid) tone.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|p=71}}</ref> Vowels are mid tone in none-tonemic varieties.

Not all types of syllables have a distinction between the two tones:

* All long vowels distinguish the two tones.
* Tautosyllabic stressed {{IPA|/əɾ/}} (i.e., {{IPA|/əɾ/}} not directly followed by a vowel in the same word) can also distinguish the two tones. It is considered "long" for this purpose, for example ''pȓstnica'' ('phalanx') with high/falling tone vs. ''pŕstanəc'' ('finger') with low/rising tone.
* For the schwa {{IPA|/ə/}} (when not part of the {{IPA|/əɾ/}} combination), the two tones are mostly in complementary distribution: it is circumflex in final syllables and acute elsewhere. This is the only case where a stressed short acute vowel can occur.
* All other stressed short vowels are always realised with a circumflex tone. They are mostly restricted to final syllables.

The non-tonemic system is identical to the tonemic system above in terms of vowel length and stress, but lacks any phonemic tone. This means that, for those dialects, the first four rows merge, as do the next three. Similarly, for speakers who do not distinguish short and long vowels, the first and third rows merge, as do the second and fourth. An exception to this is the traditional {{IPA|/á/}}, which does not merge with {{IPA|/áː/}}. Instead, the former is realized as {{IPA|[ʌ́]}}.<ref name="jurgec2005912" />

=== Secondary stress ===
Longer words, particularly loanwords, also have secondary stress. It always appears in words and word clusters when words do not have primary stress. Secondary stressed is usually every second syllable before and after the syllable with primary stress; e.g., ''aerofotogrametrija'' {{IPA|[ʔaˈɛ́ːrɔˌfotoˌgɾameˈtríːjà]}} '[[aerophotogrammetry]]'. Secondarily stressed open-mid vowels also become close-mid.<ref>{{Harvcoltxt|Jurgec|2007b|pp=164–184}}</ref>
